# Photo Request Guide

When and how to ask tenants for photos during maintenance intake and triage. Photos save vendor trips, improve diagnostic accuracy, and create documentation for the work order.

## When to Request Photos

**Always request photos for:**

- Any visible damage (water stains, cracks, mold, broken hardware)
- Leaks (location, severity, damage so far)
- Pest sightings (identification determines treatment)
- Mold or discoloration (size, color, and surface type matter)
- Lock and door issues (type of lock, visible damage, alignment)
- Window problems (hardware type, crack pattern, seal failure)
- Appliance error codes or display screens
- Anything the tenant describes as "weird looking"

**Don't request photos for:**

- Issues that are clearly described and don't have a visual component (no hot water, disposal not running, breaker won't stay on)
- Life-safety emergencies (don't delay action for documentation)
- Situations where the tenant is stressed or upset and adding a photo request will escalate frustration
- Issues the tenant already described with enough detail to classify and dispatch

**Judgment call:**

If a photo would be helpful but the tenant seems annoyed or the issue is straightforward enough to dispatch without one, skip it. A vendor can document on-site. Photos are a tool, not a requirement.

## How to Ask

Keep the request specific. "Can you send me a photo?" is too vague. The tenant won't know what to photograph or how.

**Be specific about what to photograph:**

- "Can you send me a photo of where the water is coming from?"
- "Can you get a photo of the mold? Try to include the whole area so I can see how much wall it covers."
- "Can you snap a photo of the lock? I want to see the type so I send the right person."

**Ask for scale when size matters:**

- "If you can, put a coin or your hand next to it for scale. That helps me gauge the size."
- Mold, cracks, water damage, and pest damage all benefit from scale reference.

**Ask for multiple angles when relevant:**

- "Can you get one from straight on and one from the side? That'll help me see if it's raised or flat."
- Usually only needed for structural issues, mold, or unusual damage.

**Ask for video when motion matters:**

- Running toilet (sound of the running, visible water movement)
- Unusual HVAC sounds (rattling, clicking, cycling on and off)
- Leaks that are intermittent
- Appliance behavior that's hard to describe

Keep video requests short: "A quick 10-second video of what it sounds like would be really helpful."

## What to Do with Photos

**During triage:**

- Use the photo to refine your diagnosis. A photo of a water stain on the ceiling tells you more than "there's water on my ceiling."
- Compare the photo to the KB entry's diagnostic decision tree. Does the photo match the described symptoms?
- If the photo changes your classification (what looked like a minor issue is actually significant), adjust your response accordingly.

**In the work order:**

- Attach all tenant-provided photos to the work order. This gives the vendor context before arriving.
- Note what each photo shows: "Photo 1: water stain on kitchen ceiling, approx 2ft diameter. Photo 2: close-up showing active dripping."

**For documentation:**

- Photos establish the condition at the time of the report. This matters for insurance claims, liability questions, and tracking whether an issue is getting worse.
- Always note when photos were taken (or at least when they were received).

## If the Tenant Can't Send Photos

Some tenants don't have smartphones, aren't tech-savvy, or just don't want to deal with it. That's fine.

- "No worries. I'll make sure the vendor knows to document it when they arrive."
- Don't push. Don't make the tenant feel bad. Just dispatch and rely on the vendor to document.
- If the issue is urgent enough to dispatch without photos, do it. Documentation is secondary to resolution.

## Photo Quality Tips (If the Tenant Asks)

If a tenant sends a photo that's too dark, blurry, or doesn't show what you need, you can gently ask for another:

- "Thanks for sending that. The photo's a little dark, could you try again with the lights on or the flash? I just want to make sure I'm seeing it right."
- Don't ask more than once. If the second attempt still doesn't help, move on.

Tips you can share if the tenant asks how to take a good photo:

- Turn on the lights in the room
- Use the phone's flash if needed
- Get close enough that the issue fills most of the frame
- For size reference, put a coin, pen, or your hand next to the area
- Hold the phone steady (brace your elbow against your body)
